How to Plumb a Rain Shower Head in Lodi, NJ Homes.

A rain head is more than a fixture swap. The supply line, the valve and the flow all need to suit it.

Written by Lodi Pro Plumbing / Published October 15, 2026

Call (973) 488-5410.

A rain shower head hangs flat above you and lets water fall in a wide, gentle pattern. Many people want one, and many assume it is a simple swap for the old shower head. Sometimes it is, when the rain head attaches to the existing arm.

More often it means new pipe in the wall or ceiling, a different valve and a check on water flow. This guide explains how the plumbing works so you can plan the job and judge a quote.

Because a rain head sprays a large area, it also changes how the bathroom behaves. Steam, splashing and drying time all increase, and the plumbing should account for that.

Two ways to install a rain head

The arm swap is the least invasive. A ceiling mount is the most demanding, because the supply pipe must reach the ceiling without a path from the wall. That can mean opening drywall and, in a second-floor bathroom, working above the ceiling below.

Check the valve first

The valve behind the wall controls temperature and flow. Many older valves are basic and cannot split flow between two outlets. A rain head with a hand shower needs a diverter or a valve with multiple ports. If the valve is worn, replacing it while the wall is open is cheaper than opening the wall again later.

Pressure-balancing valves keep the water temperature steadier when someone flushes a toilet or runs a tap elsewhere. A thermostatic valve holds a set temperature more precisely and is common with multi-outlet showers. The right type depends on the fixtures you choose and how you use them.

Flow rate and water pressure

A large rain head spreads water over a wide area, so it can feel weak on low pressure. Federal rules limit new shower heads to a set flow per minute, and many rain heads are made to meet it. If your home has low pressure, a rain head will feel gentler than you expect.

Before buying, test the pressure and flow. A pressure gauge on an outdoor hose bib gives a reading. A bucket and a timer under the existing shower give the flow in gallons per minute. If the numbers are low, the cause could be a clogged line, a partly closed valve or old galvanized pipe, and the answer may involve repiping sections of the house.

Supply pipe and the wall

The supply pipe to a rain head should be sized to deliver enough flow. A half-inch line is typical for one head. A shower with several outlets may need a larger line to prevent a drop in flow when all of them run. The pipe also needs solid support. A heavy head on a long arm puts a load on the fitting, and a loose fitting can leak inside the wall.

Fittings in a wall should be tested before the wall is closed. A pressure test shows leaks that are invisible once tile goes up. Ask the plumber to test, and leave the wall open until the test passes.

The drain, the curb and splashing

A rain head puts more water on the floor of the shower than a standard head. Check the drain: it should handle the flow without pooling, and a sluggish drain will fill the pan. If the drain slows, drain cleaning helps, and the pan slope should be checked too.

Keep spray inside the enclosure. A wider spray needs a glass panel or a curtain that is long enough, and a waterproof wall behind the tile. Splash damage in the wall is a common cause of mold and rot.

Hard water and rain shower nozzles in Lodi

Rain heads have many small nozzles, and minerals clog small openings. The Passaic Valley Water Commission reports hardness between 106 and 242 ppm at its Little Falls plant, and Veolia reports 210 ppm in its Hackensack system. Water at those levels leaves white scale on nozzles and slowly narrows the spray.

Choose a head with rubber or silicone nozzles that flex clean with a thumb rub. Soak the head in vinegar from time to time to dissolve scale, as the maker allows. If you live in Maywood or Hasbrouck Heights, which Veolia lists in the same system as Hackensack, the same advice applies.

Hot water supply

A rain head uses a lot of hot water per minute. If the water heater is small, the shower may run cold before you finish. Check the heater's capacity and the distance from the heater to the bathroom. A long run of pipe wastes water while it warms. A tankless unit or a larger tank can solve a supply shortage. A recirculation line cuts the wait on long runs.

When to call a plumber

Hire a plumber when the plan changes the walls, the valve or the supply pipe. A plumber can also advise on code requirements. New Jersey's plumbing subcode adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2021 edition, and the borough's Building Department handles permits for work that changes the plumbing. Ask whether your job needs one.

Rain shower questions

Can I put a rain head on my existing shower arm?

Often yes, if the arm is strong and high enough. Check the fitting for leaks after you tighten it.

Why does my rain head feel weak?

The head spreads water over a wide area, and low pressure makes that obvious. Test the flow before you buy and check for scale in the nozzles.

Do I need a new valve for a rain head?

Not always. If you want a hand shower or several outlets, you probably need a valve that can split the flow.

Will a rain head raise my water bill?

It can if you stay in longer. Federal limits cap the flow of new heads, so the effect depends on use.

How do I clean mineral scale off the nozzles?

Soak the head in white vinegar if the maker allows it, then rinse and rub the nozzles. Rubber nozzles clean more easily.

Code basis. Plumbing work in New Jersey follows the state plumbing subcode, N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, which adopts the 2021 National Standard Plumbing Code. Ask which permits apply to your job and confirm with the Lodi Building Department.
